# The science-reel format

Reverse-engineered from 12 @trainbloom reels (Tony, 1.2M followers). Every number below is
measured from those 12 scripts, not estimated. The format is unusually rigid — that rigidity
is the point, and it is what makes it reproducible.

**The premise of the format:** take a belief the audience already holds, prove it is
incomplete rather than stupid, and pay it off with a named mechanism plus one absurd
benchmark number. Never "5 tips for X."

**Write to the word count, not to a duration.** A script that overshoots will not survive
the edit; cut it on the page, not in the timeline.

**On length — the corpus ceiling is not your ceiling.** The twelve reels run 69-90s, but
that is @trainbloom's habit, not a rule of the format. In practice **90s is comfortable,
1:45 is the working target, 2:00 is the hard ceiling.** What actually caps the length is
whether the mechanism still earns its runtime, not a stopwatch. Scale the word count with
it: ~270 words at 80s, ~360 at 105s, ~410 at 120s.

What this format never does is go *short*. Nothing in the set is under 69 seconds. The long
runtime is what makes room for a mechanism, and the mechanism is what earns the save.

## The spine

Seven beats, always in this order.

| # | Beat | Length | Shot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook** | 1-14s | borrowed clip or prop |
| 2 | **Pivot** | 3-5s | face-cam |
| 3 | **Mechanism** | 15-20s | graphics |
| 4 | **Stakes / proof** | 15-25s | graphics |
| 5 | **THE TURN** | 3-5s | face-cam |
| 6 | **Resolution** | 15-25s | graphics |
| 7 | **Landing** | 5-7s | face-cam |

Face-cam is punctuation between graphic sections, never the body of the video. Three
quarters of the screen time is custom animation. Every return to face-cam carries either a
rhetorical question or a turn — never new information.

### The two halves, and the second hook that separates them

Underneath the seven beats the video is really **two movements**: everything before the turn
sets the problem up, everything after it resolves it. The turn is the seam — and it works as
a **second hook**, the line that buys the back half of the runtime after the opening hook has
been spent.

**Mark that seam with a change of music track, not with a sound effect.** The bed switches
there, with a crossfade of about 1.5s, and the second track sits *louder* than the first. The
viewer never analyses it — they feel that something changed. It carries the structure better
than any whoosh can.

**The seam is a beat, not a timestamp.** In one measured edit it landed at 49% of the runtime,
which is a coincidence of that script, not a target. Write the second hook where the argument
needs it; the edit follows the writing, and the music goes wherever the second hook went.

So when you write, know which half each line belongs to. If you cannot say which line is the
second hook, the script has no turn yet. See the `montage-capcut` skill for how it is cut.

### What the beats actually are

Underneath the seven beats there is one argument in four positions. Everything else is
staging:

1. **What you already believe** — the myth
2. **Why that is incomplete** — the correction
3. **But do not swing too far the other way** — the block
4. **What is actually true** — the answer

Worked through `DW6rQ6PDiGs` (cardio vs lifting, 118K likes), which is the cleanest example
in the set:

| Beat | Time | The actual words | What it is doing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0:00 | *[borrowed clip]* "cardio doesn't shape you… weightlifting does" / "i know." | Plants the claim. Someone else says it, so he does not have to defend it yet. |
| **Pivot** | 0:07 | "She is right… But why?" | Agrees, *then* turns the claim into a question. The promise: stay and I will explain. |
| **Mechanism** | 0:10 | "When you lose weight it comes from one of two places: body fat, or lean mass like muscle. And that balance is where shape comes from." | The invisible moving parts. Not evidence — the *how it works*. |
| **Proof** | 0:19 | Two groups, same diet, one lifted and one did cardio. Cardio lost more weight — 9 lbs of it muscle. Lifters lost 0 lbs of muscle, so lost the most fat. | Evidence the mechanism is real. Now the viewer believes it. |
| **Turn** | 0:52 | "So lifting weights is clearly the better option, right?" — **"Well, not quite…"** | Blocks the over-correction the viewer just made on their own. |
| **Resolution** | 0:56 | A third group did both — most weight lost, most fat lost, and they gained muscle. | The actual answer. The payoff. |
| **Landing** | 1:07 | "You do need to be lifting weights… but that doesn't mean cardio is useless. A balance is usually what's best." | Permission. Lowers the stakes and ends warm. |

**Pivot.** Two moves in three seconds: side with the viewer, then open a gap. The agreement
is the load-bearing half — see rule 5. It never introduces information. Prop-hook videos
merge the hook and the pivot, which is why a prop hook runs 1-4s and a react hook 6-14s.

**Mechanism.** The invisible machinery that makes the claim true. The test: the viewer says
*"yeah, but how?"* — the mechanism is the answer.

| A claim (not a mechanism) | The mechanism |
|---|---|
| "Muscle comes back faster" | Myonuclei get added as you build muscle and stay behind when it shrinks, so the cell keeps the infrastructure |
| "Diet Coke is fine" | Everything has a dose threshold, and the ADI is set 100× below the highest dose that showed no harm |
| "Processed doesn't mean bad" | Processed just means *changed*; EPG is a fat the body cannot fully absorb, so it tastes like fat at 0.7 cal/g instead of 9 |
| "Cardio doesn't shape you" | Weight loss comes from fat or from muscle, and shape is the ratio between them |

It needs two properties, both required: a **name** you can put on screen, and it must be
**drawable** — this beat is what becomes an animated composition. If you cannot draw it, you
do not have a mechanism yet, you have a claim.

**Stakes / proof.** One slot doing two jobs: *why should I believe you* and *why should I
care*. Proof is the study, the numbers, the experiment. Stakes is what goes wrong if you get
it wrong. Most of the set leads with proof; `DbtFjC6OqBG` leads with stakes (a low VO2 max
predicts death more strongly than smoking) and only then gives the protocol. Lead with proof
when the claim is surprising, with stakes when the claim is boring but important.

**The turn.** Not a question about your own hook — a question about **the conclusion the
viewer has just reached by themselves**. That ordering is the whole trick: they have to
arrive at the wrong conclusion first, which is why the turn sits after the proof and never
before it. Forty seconds of evidence that lifting beats cardio makes the viewer think *so
never do cardio*; the turn catches that. It is a guardrail against over-learning, and it is
what makes the balanced landing feel earned instead of mealy-mouthed.

**Resolution.** Not a second counter-argument — there is only one counter-argument in the
video and that is the turn. The resolution is **the answer that survives it**, and it is the
longest beat at ~29% of runtime. It is the part people screenshot.

**Landing.** Not a summary; it never restates the argument. It grants permission and lowers
the stakes so the video ends warm. Shape: concede the loss, then name what survives it.

## The five rules

### 1. Only ever pick a contested belief

Test: can you write the premise as **"Everyone thinks X. Actually Y — and here is the
mechanism"**? If not, it is not this format.

All 12 do this. None is a tips list, a routine, or a product review.

### 2. The mechanism is mandatory, and it must have a name

| Reel | The named mechanism |
|---|---|
| Muscle memory | **myonuclei** — cell control centres that survive atrophy |
| Processed food | **EPG fat** — plant fat the body cannot fully absorb |
| Diet Coke | **NOAEL / ADI** — the 100x regulatory safety buffer |
| Calorie deficit | **TDEE components** — BMR, NEAT, TEF, exercise |
| Ashwagandha | **cortisol suppression**, benchmarked against metyrapone |
| Skinny-fat | **body recomposition** — fat layer sitting over muscle |
| Metabolic adaptation | the four-part metabolism stack shrinking |
| VO2 max | the oxygen path: lungs → heart → muscle |

A fact without a mechanism is advice. A mechanism is teaching, and teaching is what gets
saved and sent. If you cannot name the mechanism you do not have the video yet — go
research, do not start writing.

### 3. The turn — the second hook, at 57%

Present the obvious conclusion, let the viewer agree with it, then reverse it. This is the
most consistent device in the set and it lands just past the midpoint.

Verbatim from the corpus:

- "So lifting weights is clearly the better option, right?" → **"Well, not quite…"**
- "…that's still 200mg of a chemical you don't want in your body, right?" → **"Well, not really."**
- "So as long as you're not getting it from these, could nicotine actually be good for you?" → **"Well, not quite."**
- "So why doesn't everyone take it? **Well, there is one big problem with it.**"
- "By now, I think everyone agrees that nicotine is horrible for you. **But, what if everyone's been wrong?**"
- "But that's not really surprising. **What came next, is…**"
- "**But the crazier part is** that researchers couldn't find a ceiling to it either."
- "What if that's because **you're trying to solve the wrong problem?**"

Mark it in the audio: a record scratch or a bass drop. Nothing else in the video gets one.

### 4. Every number gets an absurd benchmark

A bare statistic is forgettable. Each one in the set is anchored to something the viewer
already has a feel for:

- 2,500 cans of Diet Coke per day to reach actual harm
- one Starbucks coffee gets you **50x closer** to caffeine's limit than a Diet Coke does to aspartame's
- ashwagandha drops cortisol 67%; the prescription drug manages 50-70%
- the David bar out-proteins chicken breast, Greek yogurt, lean beef, salmon and eggs
- 6 weeks to regain what took 20 to build — **3x faster**
- low VO2 max predicts death more strongly than smoking, coronary artery disease, or type-2 diabetes
- the cardio group lost more weight — **9 lbs of it was muscle**

Never leave a number bare. If you cannot benchmark it, cut it.

**And some numbers cannot be benchmarked at all — those are never spoken.** A correlation
coefficient, a p-value, a Cohen's *d*: these have no everyday scale, so there is nothing to
anchor them to. Caught by the native speaker reading a finished script aloud:

> ❌ « Le régime choisi prédit la perte à zéro virgule zéro sept. »
> — *"0,07 quoi ? c'est quoi l'unité ?"*

The instinct is to rescue it with a second line — « même échelle, de zéro à un » — and that
is the trap: it stacks a second technical sentence on the first, and the viewer leaves. **A
benchmark has to be a thing the listener already owns.** They know what three kilos weigh.
They know what half a group walking out looks like. They do not know what 0,60 is worth.

The fix is never to explain the statistic. It is to say the same finding in the study's own
countable units — people, kilos, calories, minutes:

> ✅ « Quatre régimes, un an. Atkins deux virgule un kilos, la Zone trois virgule deux,
> Weight Watchers trois, Ornish trois virgule trois. » — *the same r=0,07, in kilos*
> ✅ « Sur quarante personnes par groupe, vingt ont fini chez Ornish. » — *the dropout, as people*

The coefficient can stay on screen in a source strip, or live in the caption. It never goes
in the mouth.

**The audit is mechanical**: grep the narration for *corrélation*, *p =*, *taille d'effet*,
and any spelled-out decimal. One pass over a finished 8-minute script turned up nine such
shots across five chapters — every one of them written after the "benchmark every number"
rule was already in this file. The rule was being satisfied on paper by a benchmark that
was itself unreadable.

### 5. Validate before you correct

The first host line after a react hook is agreement, not a dunk:

> "She might actually be right? But, here's how you fix it."
> "She is right… But why?"
> "You know someone did that?"
> "Peter Attia told me VO2 max is the single most important metric… I think absolutely he's right."

He never opens by telling the viewer, or the stitched creator, that they are wrong. The
correction arrives at the turn, forty seconds later, once the viewer is invested. This is a
tone rule, and breaking it is the fastest way to make the format read like every other
debunk account.

## Hooks

Eight of twelve are **react**, four are **prop**. Performance does not separate them — the
top four videos are three react and one prop, and both the best and the worst video in the
set use a react hook. Choose on what the topic gives you, not on which is "better."

### A. React / stitch — 6-14s of borrowed clip

Split-screen or green-screen, host visible, usually holding a yellow phone. Then a hard cut,
a whoosh, and the music drops to full.

Four sub-types, all present in the set:

| Sub-type | Example | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Named authority | Sam Sulek on thermodynamics | 21.8x, 7.5M |
| Shock visual | post-ACL leg atrophy | 285K likes |
| Peer claim to validate | "cardio doesn't shape you" | 118K likes |
| Comedy skit | "is that ashwagandha?" | 47K likes |

The borrowed clip must make a **claim**, not merely be entertaining. The whole video is the
answer to it.

### B. Prop / pedestal — 1-4s

White pedestal box, locked-off camera, physical products. Items swap in the same screen
position between hard cuts, each with a pop SFX. The camera never moves, so the swap reads
as magic.

### The six hook sentence shapes

1. **Consensus flip** — "By now, I think everyone agrees that nicotine is horrible for you. But, what if everyone's been wrong?"
2. **Both-are-true paradox** — "This food is processed… but, so is this food?"
3. **Versus question** — "Aspartame vs. full sugar coke — which one's actually worse for you?"
4. **Negation reframe** — "You know a calorie deficit doesn't just mean eat less food, right?"
5. **Validate and deepen** — "She is right… But why?"
6. **Story tease** — "You know someone did that? But with Twinkies to prove a point. And weight isn't the only thing they lost."

## Line-level craft

**Uptalk.** Statements are delivered with rising intonation so they land as open loops — they
transcribe as questions: *"it was changed?"*, *"than you'd think…"*, *"be pretty simple?"*,
*"is pretty smart"*. Aim for one unresolved line every 10-15 seconds.

**Rhythm.** 14 words on average, but the average hides the pattern: a long explanatory
sentence followed by a fragment. *"Well, not quite."* *"But why?"* *"Not really."* *"For
everything."*

**The `which is how / which is why` connector.** Chains mechanism to consequence without
starting a new sentence, which keeps the pace up:

> "…which is how foods that have it can still have the same taste that fat adds to food"
> "Which is why size comes back so much faster."
> "…why some researchers are calling it one of the most exciting new tools we have"

**Second person throughout.** "your body", "someone my size", "you would need", "if you're
avoiding". Never "one" or "people" where "you" will do.

**Concrete over categorical.** Not "a large study" but "research done on over 1.1 million
people". Not "significantly" but "67%".

## On-screen captions

- Bold sans-serif, centred, word-by-word or 2-3 word chunks, popping in on the beat
- **Verbatim** match to the narration during face-cam
- Exactly **one** highlighted word per sentence — more, and the emphasis means nothing
- Colour carries meaning and stays consistent within a video:
  - **yellow / purple** — emphasis, or the key term being introduced
  - **green** — the solution, the thing that works, the correct option
  - **red** — the myth, the risk, the loss
- Graphics use a different, blockier face (pixel or heavy sans) so the two layers never read
  as the same voice

## Sound design

**Music.** One bed, no changes. It sits low under the borrowed clip and **drops to full on
the cut to face-cam** at 0:07-0:14 — that drop is the moment the video actually starts.
Genres used across the set: lo-fi hip-hop, retro synthwave, 8-bit lo-fi, upbeat pop. Ducked
roughly 15dB under the voice.

**Effects.** ~16 per video, one per graphic element entering. The palette is retro arcade:
pop, ding, buzz, whoosh, error chime, coin, riser, bass drop, cash register, crunch,
heartbeat, slide whistle.

**Reserve the record scratch and the bass drop for the turn.** They appear once per video.

## Graphics grammar

Two visual dialects — 8-bit pixel art on dark, and flat minimalist vector on white — but
identical grammar. Eight recurring devices, each appearing more than once:

1. **The splitting bar** — one bar divides into labelled, colour-coded segments with percentages
2. **The populating list** — items appear one at a time, each with its own ding
3. **Red X / green check** pairs for wrong-option vs right-option
4. **Stick-figure populations** — study cohorts as rows of little people
5. **The timeline** — weeks drawn left to right, with an on/off marker sliding
6. **The leaderboard** — ranked bars filling bottom to top
7. **Before / after** side by side at the same scale
8. **The absurd grid** — the camera scrolls up past thousands of copies of one object

Pick two or three per video. Reusing them is the visual signature; inventing a new device
every time destroys it.

## The landing

Two endings exist in the set, and they do not perform the same.

**Philosophical landing — 9 of 12, and all four biggest videos.** No CTA. It concedes
something, then names the durable truth. It de-escalates the video's own energy, which is
why it loops cleanly back to the hook.

> "It's less about what you're taking, and more about the form it comes in."
> "The people who keep losing weight aren't lucky, they just learned how to keep adjusting."
> "It just means you don't have to be afraid of the ones that aren't."
> "So yeah, losing muscle sucks, but your body doesn't forget it."
> "You're really solving a problem that was never there."

Shape: **concede the loss, then name what survives it.**

**Bio CTA — 3 of 12.** "I have free calculators in my bio", "notes in the caption you can
save for later."

In this sample all three CTA videos sit in the bottom half by likes, and the four biggest end
with no CTA at all. With n=12 that is a strong prior rather than proof — the CTA videos also
happen to cover the least contested topics — but **default to the philosophical landing** and
spend a CTA deliberately, not by habit.

## The caption is half the format

Median 204 words. One idea per line, blank line between. Five parts, in order:

1. **Title line** — the video's claim, restated flat: *"Muscle memory is a real thing"*
2. **Immediate qualifier** — *"Just not in the way people usually mean it…"* / *"Not quite 🥲💔"*
3. **The expansion** — numbered lessons, or the mechanism detail the video had no room for
4. **The honest caveat** — actively limits the claim
5. **PMIDs / DOIs**, then two or three hashtags at most

The caveat is not hedging — it is the trust engine. The video is confident and simplified;
the caption is where the receipts and the limits live:

> "Now this is just one guy who did a self experiment, you can't use this as evidence"
> "…they do eventually fade with prolonged inactivity (so it's not a permanent free pass)"
> "Short term health improvements like this are not the same thing as long term health outcomes"

Reserving genuinely useful specifics for the caption — dosing protocols, the Norwegian 4x4,
the three lessons — is what converts a view into a save.

## Writing loop

Do it in this order. The middle is the easiest part, and writing it first is how scripts end
up at 400 words with no turn.

1. **Pick the belief.** Write it as "Everyone thinks X. Actually Y."
2. **Find the mechanism** and its name. No name, no video.
3. **Find the benchmark number.** The absurd comparison.
4. **Write the turn** — the reversal line, verbatim.
5. **Write the landing** — concede, then name what survives.
6. **Write the hook** — now that you know what it has to set up.
7. **Fill the middle** to 270 words total.
8. **Break into shots** at ~3s, ~25 of them, with 4 returns to face-cam.
9. **Annotate** each shot: `TEXT` / `SFX` / `MUSIC` / `CAM` / `FX`.
10. **Write the caption**, all five parts, caveat included.
11. **Count the words.** Cut to 270. Read it aloud against a stopwatch.

Save with `project_update(script=...)` in bracket format.

## French adaptation

Reference: `DTdVpZljqZh` (Twinkie diet) — the account's biggest video, 21.8x / 7.5M.

### Rule zero: the French has to be *correct French*

This outranks every timing target below. A line that scans, hits the beat and lands the
rhythm is still worthless if it does not mean anything. **Do not translate the English
structure — write the French sentence from scratch, then check it.**

The failure mode is specific and it is easy to miss, because a broken sentence can sound
perfectly fluent. Real example that shipped into a script:

> ❌ « Un déficit calorique, c'est pas manger moins que ce que tu bouges. »

It reads fine out loud. It means nothing. *Manger moins* is a quantity of food; *ce que tu
bouges* is an amount of movement. **The two sides of the comparison share no unit**, so
there is nothing to compare. You cannot eat less than you move.

It came from inverting the English — *"a calorie deficit isn't just if you exercise more
than you eat"* — where the loose comparison survives because English tolerates it. Flipped
into French, it collapses.

Either of these works:

> ✅ « c'est pas juste **bouger plus que ce que tu manges** » — keeps the original's shape
> ✅ « c'est pas manger moins que ce que tu **dépenses** » — both sides in calories, airtight

**The check, on every line: do both sides of any comparison share a unit?** Calories against
calories, time against time, movement against movement. If not, rewrite.

### Rule zero, second half: a unit is not a thing you can act on

The same category error has a second form, and it is harder to see because there is no
comparison to inspect — just a relative pronoun quietly turning a measurement into an object.
Caught in a shipped draft:

> ❌ « pas dans le dessert, mais dans les neuf calories par gramme **que tu verses sans les
> mâcher**. »

*Que* makes « neuf calories par gramme » the direct object of *verser*, and *les* makes it the
object of *mâcher*. But **nine calories per gram is a density, not a substance.** You cannot
pour a ratio, and you certainly cannot chew one. What gets poured is the oil; nine calories per
gram is a property of it.

> ✅ « pas dans le dessert, mais dans **ce que tu verses sans le mâcher** — neuf calories par
> gramme. »

The object is now a substance, *le* agrees with it, and the density lands as an apposition —
which is also where it hits hardest, at the end of the sentence.

**The check: every rate, density, ratio and percentage is a property, never an actor and never
a target.** If a unit is the subject of a verb of action or the object of a physical one,
rewrite. Watch for `kcal/g`, `%`, correlations, and anything with *par* in it — those are the
words that slip into object position while sounding perfectly natural.

Note that the same sentence read fine to three separate model passes and was caught by the
native speaker on first listen. Fluency is not the test; **grammatical role is.**

### The four other traps, all seen in real drafts

| ❌ | Why it breaks | ✅ |
|---|---|---|
| « Elle **fait grossir** la plus grosse part » | *faire grossir* means "make you fat". In a weight-loss video you have just said the opposite of your point. | « elle **augmente** la plus grosse part » |
| « Ce que tu **bouges** en dehors du sport » | *bouger* is not transitive like that — *ce que tu bouges* = "the thing you move". | « ce que tu **dépenses** en bougeant » |
| « **Dix minutes de marche**, rester debout, prendre les escaliers. » | A verbless list. English carries it; spoken French sounds like reading a slide. **This is what makes the delivery feel strange.** | Govern it: « bouger hors de la salle : **marcher** dix minutes après les repas, **rester** debout, **prendre** les escaliers » |
| « trois à cinq cents calories » | Spoken, this is 3–500 or 300–500. The ear cannot tell. | « **trois cents** à cinq cents » |

Keep the list items grammatically parallel — all infinitives, or all noun phrases, never a
mix. And read every line aloud before it goes in the script: the verbless-list problem is
inaudible on the page and obvious in the mouth.

### Timing

French needs roughly 15-20% more words than English for the same idea. With the ceiling at
2:00 that is no longer the squeeze it was at 90s — but the *rate* still matters. Target
**185-195 wpm**, and check every beat individually: the global average hides beats that are
physically unsayable. A script averaging 184 wpm had ten of its twenty-eight shots above
205, one at 360. Compute words ÷ shot duration for each shot, not for the whole.

**Captions must chunk wider.** French has fewer monosyllables, so word-by-word captions
strobe. Use 2-4 word chunks. Keep one highlight per sentence.

**Register: tutoiement, always.** The format is a friend explaining something. *Vous* makes
it a lecture and kills it.

**Typography:** French takes a narrow space before `? ! : ;` and inside `« »`. Set this in
the caption tool, or it reads as wrong to native speakers.

**Units:** he already speaks metric for mg/kg/g, but pounds must convert — 27 lbs → 12 kg,
9 lbs → 4 kg. Keep "calories".

### The turn, in French

| English | French |
|---|---|
| "Well, not quite…" | « Enfin… pas vraiment. » |
| "Well, not really." | « Pas vraiment. » |
| "But what if everyone's been wrong?" | « Et si tout le monde se trompait ? » |
| "…right?" | « …non ? » / « …c'est ça ? » |
| "But here's the thing." | « Sauf qu'il y a un truc. » |
| "But the crazier part is…" | « Et le plus fou, c'est que… » |
| "What came next, is…" | « Ce qui a suivi, par contre… » |

### The six hook shapes, in French

1. « Tout le monde te dit que [X] est mauvais pour toi. Et si c'était faux ? »
2. « Ça, c'est [transformé]. Mais ça aussi ? »
3. « [A] ou [B] — lequel est vraiment le pire ? »
4. « Tu sais qu'un déficit calorique, ça veut pas juste dire manger moins ? »
5. « Elle a raison… mais pourquoi ? »
6. « Tu sais que quelqu'un l'a vraiment fait ? Et c'est pas que le poids qu'il a perdu. »

### The landing, in French

Keep the concede-then-name shape:

> « Donc oui, perdre du muscle c'est nul. Mais ton corps, lui, il oublie pas. »
> « C'est moins une question de ce que tu prends, et plus de la forme sous laquelle tu le prends. »

### The one real obstacle

**The react hook needs French-language source clips.** A stitched English clip loses a French
audience in the first two seconds, and that is 8 of the 12 hooks gone. Two options:

- **Build a seed list of French creators** in the niche and sweep it with
  `instagram_account_outliers` / `tiktok_account_outliers`. Neither platform offers free
  hashtag, keyword or trending search, so research has to start from named accounts — see the
  `video` skill for that limit in full.
- **Lean on prop hooks**, which are language-neutral and are 4 of the 12 anyway. The pedestal
  swap and the versus setup translate with no source material at all.

Citations stay as PMIDs and DOIs — those are language-neutral — but write the caption body in
French.

## Scaling to long form — what breaks at 9 minutes

Everything above was measured on 69-90s reels. Pushing the same spine to a 9-minute YouTube
video does **not** mean writing more of the same: the reel settings actively produce an
unwatchable long-form script. The eight rules below all come from a native speaker reading a
finished 8-minute draft aloud and rejecting it — *« la rédaction est très robotique, très
inhumaine »*, *« on dirait écrit par un enfant »*.

**The root cause is the density.** 185 wpm and 3-second shots work when the viewer already
knows the creator and the topic, and when rewatching is normal. On nine minutes they watch
**once, linearly, and have to be taught.** There is literally no room to explain. Drop to
**~160-175 wpm and 3-5s shots**, and accept the runtime that follows.

### 1. A metaphor is a micro-module, not a set

This is the failure that cost the most rewrites. The bathtub image was installed in chapter 1
and then used for the rest of the video — *« quel robinet coule le plus vite ? »* — which
replaces a real question about calories with a riddle about a metaphor.

**Install it in 15-20 seconds, then close it out loud and never return:**

> ✅ « Voilà. C'est ça, un déficit calorique. Rien d'autre. »
> ✅ « Et maintenant qu'on a dit ça, **on peut oublier la baignoire.** »

After that line: calories, food, stomach, in plain words. Grep the remaining chapters for the
metaphor's nouns before shipping — three survived the first pass.

### 2. Anchor every term of the metaphor out loud

Never assume the mapping is obvious. *« un robinet : ce que tu manges »* is not an anchor;
it's a label. The anchors that actually worked are full sentences: « le niveau de l'eau,
**c'est** ton poids », « ce robinet, **c'est** un aliment », « cette fuite, **c'est** ce que
ton corps brûle ». Without them the viewer cannot connect the image to their own life, and
every later callback lands on nothing.

### 3. Never tell the viewer what they concluded

*« Et là tu viens de conclure tout seul… »* — banned. It tells them what they think, and if
they didn't think it, you have lost them. **Voice the thought in their place, quietly**, and
show it in quotation marks on screen so no isolated frame reads as the video's own claim:

> ✅ « Alors on se dit : d'accord. C'est juste une question de tenir. »
> ✅ « Sauf que regarde qui a tenu. »

### 4. No word without context

*« Ce n'est pas ta volonté qui a lâché. C'est ta règle. »* — at six seconds in, no rule has
been established. The viewer has no idea what rule is meant. Before using a term, check it
has been introduced in this video; otherwise introduce it or pick another word.

### 5. No laboratory units

Nobody says millilitres, kilojoules, or grams of lipid out loud. **400 mL is two big glasses
of water. 1.5 L is a large bottle.** Keep calories and kilos; convert everything else to an
object the viewer has held. And a stomach does not *count* anything — it senses volume, it
reacts when it stretches.

### 6. Every sentence literally true

Compression produces sentences that scan and mean nothing. *« Tu as craqué parce que ton
estomac n'a jamais su que tu avais mangé »* is false: it knew. What did not happen is that it
was never filled enough to signal it. Read each line at face value and ask whether it is true
as written, even when it sounds good.

### 7. Write like someone talking, not like a fact sheet

The creator speaks from their own experience — « moi aussi je me suis dit ça, pendant des
années » — concedes when the evidence is thin — « honnêtement, huit personnes, c'est pas
grand-chose » — and drops a short sentence after a long one. The viewer has been lied to for
years; they are not stupid, and you are not lecturing them. **Warmth, not authority.** Ban
stacked noun phrases, dry enumerations, and the impersonal *il faut*.

### 8. Split before you compress

A 10-minute target and "explain properly" are usually incompatible, and the honest resolution
is a **split, not a squeeze**. In practice: 8 chapters that needed 18 minutes became two
9-minute videos, cut at the seam where the argument already turned (why it fails / what to do
instead). The cost is exactly two seams, and they are the same two the shorts need anyway —
a landing that **concludes** instead of opening the next chapter, and an opening that works
**cold** for someone who has not seen part one.

Watch for the scope error that makes the split necessary in the first place. Named plainly by
the author: *« on était trop gourmand dans le premier sujet »*.

## Production

Animation is done in **HyperFrames** (HTML compositions with `data-*` timing, rendered to
video) or **Remotion**; the edit is assembled in **CapCut** via its MCP server.

Which beats are code and which are camera:

| Beat | Made in |
|---|---|
| Hook | camera, or the borrowed clip |
| Pivot | camera |
| **Mechanism** | HyperFrames / Remotion |
| **Stakes / proof** | HyperFrames / Remotion |
| Turn | camera |
| **Resolution** | HyperFrames / Remotion |
| Landing | camera |

That is ~74% of the runtime as code, matching the measured face-cam share.

**Build each insert as its own short composition, not one 80-second timeline.** Inserts run
2-5s, CapCut needs them as separate clips on the timeline anyway, and per-shot files are what
`capcut_plan` places against the bracket timestamps. Render at 1080×1920. HyperFrames is
documented as strongest under 10s for motion graphics and 30-90s for narrative pieces, which
is exactly the shape of this work — a stack of short inserts inside a 75-90s whole.

**Build the eight devices once, as reusable components.** The format reuses the same visual
grammar every video — splitting bar, populating list, X/check pair, stick-figure cohort,
timeline, leaderboard, before/after, absurd grid. Parameterising those eight is most of the
production cost, and after the first video the marginal cost of an insert is writing props.
Reusing them is the visual signature; inventing new devices each time destroys it.

The workflow stays `broll_plan_init` → decide each shot → `broll_plan_set` (record the
rendered file on its shot) → `capcut_plan`. Read `capcut_reference` before hand-writing any
CapCut call; `save_draft` silently produces blank media paths without `draft_folder`.

## What does not transfer

**The animation is still the real cost.** Three quarters of every video is custom 2D
animation built to the script — writing it in code changes who does the work, not how much
there is. Without it the format collapses into a talking head with stock footage, which is a
different and much weaker video. The one thing never to substitute is stock b-roll: it breaks
the visual signature entirely, and a plain title card beats a mismatched stock clip.

**Sponsored versions underperform.** `DZK5jErOZL5` is a David protein bar integration in the
identical format, and it is the weakest prop video in the set — 28K likes against 94-106K for
the unsponsored ones. Plan for the drop rather than being surprised by it.

**Borrowed footage carries real exposure.** Eight of twelve hooks use another creator's clip.
See the `video` skill's section on using other people's footage; the short version is that
Content ID matches automatically, and a claim does not care whether your use would hold up.
Short excerpts doing real argumentative work are defensible; decoration is not.
